Keto Beat Mediterranean and Plant-Based Diets on Liver Health, Even Losing the Same Amount of Weight

A controlled trial gave three groups the exact same 10% weight loss on three different diets. Liver health still came out uneven — and keto pulled meaningfully ahead.

R

Rashtriya Gaurav

7 min read · Sep 4, 2026

For years, the standard advice for obesity-related liver problems has been simple: lose weight, any way that works for you, and the metabolic benefits will follow. A new randomized trial out of Washington University School of Medicine says that's only half true. When people lost the exact same amount of weight, the diet they used to get there still mattered, and a strict ketogenic diet came out ahead on liver health specifically.

The Study Setup

Researchers led by Dr. Max C. Petersen and Dr. Samuel Klein, both at the Center for Human Nutrition, wanted to isolate a question that's surprisingly hard to answer: if two people lose the same 10% of their body weight, does it matter whether they did it on keto, Mediterranean, or a low-fat, plant-forward diet? Petersen noted that most prior research had shown people can lose weight and improve metabolic health on any number of diets, but never controlled for whether the diet itself, independent of the weight lost, changes the outcome.

The timing matters too. With GLP-1 medications now widely used for obesity, Klein pointed out that these drugs are proven to drive major weight loss, but whether what someone eats on top of that adds anything beyond the number on the scale hasn't been established.

Who Was in the Trial

The study randomized 55 adults with metabolically unhealthy obesity — meaning obesity plus prediabetes plus fatty liver — into one of three diets: a very-low-carb ketogenic diet (4% of calories from carbs), a Mediterranean diet (50% of calories from carbs), or a very-low-fat, plant-forward diet (70% of calories from carbs).

This wasn't a "here's a pamphlet, good luck" study. Every meal and snack was prepared and provided to participants, and they met weekly with a dietitian. Forty-two people completed the full protocol; average age was 43, average starting BMI was 38.9. All three groups lost around 10% of their body weight over 4 to 5 months, then held that weight for 3 to 4 weeks before final testing.

The Results: Same Weight Loss, Different Liver Outcomes

Muscle insulin sensitivity improved by roughly 50% across all three diets — essentially a tie. Liver health did not tie.

Liver fat dropped 67% on keto versus 45% on the other two diets — and prediabetes remission hit 50% on keto, compared with just 7% on the plant-forward diet.

Hepatic insulin sensitivity improved two to three times more in the keto group than in either the Mediterranean or plant-forward group. Liver fat, measured as intrahepatic triglycerides, dropped 67% on keto, versus 45% on each of the other two diets. Blood sugar control (HbA1c) improved more on keto as well. Prediabetes remission occurred in half of the keto group, compared with 29% on Mediterranean and just 7% on the plant-forward diet.

The Part That Surprised the Researchers

Keto diets carry a reputation for raising cholesterol and straining cardiovascular markers, largely because of how much saturated fat they typically involve. This trial's keto group got 23% of its calories from saturated fat, and none of the usual red flags showed up. LDL cholesterol, apolipoprotein B, and triglycerides came out statistically similar across all three diets. Muscle insulin sensitivity wasn't worse on keto either.

Petersen called that the real surprise: strong liver benefits with none of the downsides that typically make clinicians hesitant to recommend a high-fat, high-saturated-fat diet. No serious adverse events occurred in any group, and nobody dropped out due to side effects.

The Catch: This Isn't How Real Life Usually Works

Petersen was direct about the limits of the findings. This trial measured what happens when people rigorously follow one diet, with every meal handed to them, for months. That is a very different scenario from a doctor recommending a diet during a routine appointment.

"Not all people with metabolically unhealthy obesity will want to adopt a very-low-carbohydrate ketogenic diet, and many who try it will not continue it for long," he said. The diet tested was also extreme by design, under 5% of calories from carbs, so a more moderate high-fat, lower-carb approach might not produce the same effect. The results may not generalize either to people with obesity who don't already have prediabetes and fatty liver.

Petersen's takeaway: for people who need to lose weight to reverse prediabetes and fatty liver, any diet that cuts calories will help. But pound for pound, liver health specifically may improve more on a strict ketogenic approach.

Klein framed the bigger-picture implication: diet composition, not just weight loss itself, should be treated as part of the actual treatment plan for people with obesity, prediabetes, and liver disease together.

What Outside Experts Are Saying

Dr. William Yancy of Duke's Lifestyle and Weight Management Center, who wasn't involved in the study, said it reinforces earlier low-carb research but stands out for its rigor: providing all meals rather than relying on self-reported eating gives a much more confident read on what strict adherence actually does to the body. His caveat is the same tradeoff in reverse: that level of adherence is far easier to hit in a controlled trial than in everyday life. He'd like to see these diets tested alongside GLP-1 medications next, given how much those drugs already help with prediabetes and fatty liver on their own.

Dr. Esther López-García of the Autonomous University of Madrid raised a different concern: the study doesn't address food quality within each diet type. A high-fat diet built around fatty processed meats is not nutritionally equivalent to one built around olive oil and fish, even if both hit the same fat percentage. Similarly, a high-carb diet full of whole grains and vegetables is not the same as one built around sugary drinks and pastries. She pointed to Spain's national dietary guidelines, which favor a moderate-to-high intake of healthy fats and healthy carbs together, as a more balanced framework for general disease prevention.

The Bottom Line

Weight loss alone helped everyone in this trial. But when it came to liver-specific outcomes, the ketogenic diet pulled meaningfully ahead of Mediterranean and plant-forward eating, without the cholesterol or muscle-insulin downsides researchers expected going in. The tradeoff is adherence: this was diet composition tested under near-ideal conditions, not a real-world guarantee. For now, the honest summary is that any calorie-reducing diet helps a struggling liver, but keto may help it the most, if someone can actually stick with it.
