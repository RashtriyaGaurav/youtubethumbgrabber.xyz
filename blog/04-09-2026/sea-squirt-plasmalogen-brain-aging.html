Stanford-Linked Scientists Found a Sea Creature That May Reverse Signs of Aging in the Brain

A compound pulled from sea squirts restored memory, rebuilt brain connections, and calmed inflammation in aged mice — with thicker, darker hair regrowth as an unexpected bonus.

6 min read · Aug 29, 2026

Gray hair, memory lapses, slower thinking: the usual markers of aging are so familiar that most people assume they're one-way streets. A new animal study is challenging that assumption, and the compound behind it comes from an unlikely place — a marine animal already eaten across parts of Asia.

A joint team from Xi'an Jiaotong-Liverpool University, Stanford University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, and the University of Chinese Academy of Sciences ran the experiment on aged mice. The results: better memory, denser brain connections, lower brain inflammation, and, almost as a side effect, thicker, glossier, darker hair regrowth.

The Unlikely Source: Sea Squirts and a Lipid Called Plasmalogen

The animal in question is the sea squirt (Ascidiacea), sold and eaten raw in Korea (meongge) and Japan (hoya). What makes it scientifically interesting isn't the animal itself but a compound it's rich in: plasmalogens, a class of fat molecules that make up a meaningful share of cell membranes throughout the body, particularly in the brain, heart, and immune cells.

Plasmalogen levels naturally decline with age, and researchers have separately observed lower plasmalogen levels in people with Alzheimer's and Parkinson's disease. That correlation is what pushed this team to test whether restoring plasmalogens through diet could push back against brain aging directly, rather than just tracking alongside it.

The Experiment: Feeding Aged Mice a Plasmalogen-Enriched Diet

The researchers added plasmalogens to the diets of aged mice, then measured both behavioral and physical changes against aged mice on a normal diet. Lead author Professor Lei Fu said the supplemented mice didn't just stop declining — they showed reversal of existing cognitive impairment, alongside visibly thicker, glossier, and darker hair regrowth.

Memory Testing: How Much Did Learning Actually Improve?

To measure cognition, the team used the Morris water maze, a standard test where mice swim to find a hidden platform. Young mice typically learn the platform's location quickly; aged mice usually take longer, which is the standard proxy researchers use for age-related memory decline.

After five days of training, the plasmalogen-fed aged mice were reaching the platform markedly faster than untreated aged mice, performing closer to what you'd expect from younger animals.

What Changed Inside the Brain

When the researchers examined brain tissue, two differences stood out between the groups.

More, and healthier, synapses. Synapses are the junctions nerve cells use to communicate, and they're central to how memory and learning actually function at a cellular level. The plasmalogen-fed mice had a higher synapse count, and those synapses looked structurally healthier.

Lower brain inflammation. Chronic inflammation in the brain is considered a driver of neurodegeneration, and it tends to increase with age as immune regulation becomes less precise. The treated mice showed substantially less of it.

Synaptic density tends to drop as the brain ages, a process tied to declining neural plasticity — the brain's capacity to form new connections and adapt. The fact that plasmalogen-fed mice retained more synaptic capacity is the likely mechanical link to their improved test performance.

More synapses, less inflammation, and faster learning — the plasmalogen-fed mice looked biologically younger on every measure the team tracked.

The Open Question: How Exactly Do Plasmalogens Do This?

Professor Fu pointed to two possible mechanisms, neither of which is fully confirmed yet.

Direct neuroregeneration support. The team found plasmalogens raised levels of molecules that support neuron and synapse growth, meaning the compound may actively help the brain rebuild connections, not just protect existing ones. Plasmalogens may also make synaptic membranes more fluid and flexible, which could affect how well impulses transmit between neurons.

A gut-brain pathway. Plasmalogens appear to influence gut microorganisms, and a growing body of research connects gut microbiome composition to neurodegeneration through what's usually called the gut-brain axis. Fu suggested some of the cognitive benefit could be routed through this gut effect rather than acting on the brain in isolation.

Could This Translate to Humans?

Professor Fu is confident enough in the findings that he takes a plasmalogen supplement daily, and has floated oral plasmalogen supplementation as a possible strategy for improving cognitive function in older adults.

That said, this is still an animal study. Mouse results, even striking ones, don't guarantee the same effect in humans. Nobody yet knows the effective human dose, whether eating sea squirts specifically would replicate the effect, or what the safety profile looks like over years of use. That's the next phase of research, not something confirmed by this study alone.

The Bottom Line

This is one of the first studies to map, in real detail, how a dietary compound might not just slow but actively reverse markers of brain aging: more synapses, less inflammation, better memory performance, in an animal model. It's early, it's mice, and it's one team's data. But it's a genuinely new lead on a question — whether brain aging can be reversed rather than just managed — that's had very few credible answers so far.

Scientists proved how fear of health decline speeds up your aging with 700+ adults

A 2026 study led by researchers at the NYU School of Global Public Health evaluated data from 726 midlife adults to examine a counterintuitive question: Can worrying about getting older actually accelerate cellular aging?

By analyzing blood samples using advanced "epigenetic clocks," specifically DunedinPACE, which measures the real-time pace of biological aging, researchers discovered a direct, measurable link between psychological health anxiety and accelerated cellular decline.

Key Findings from the NYU Study

Not All Anxiety Ages You Equally

Researchers separated aging worries into three distinct categories: appearance, fertility, and health decline. Concerns regarding beauty and fertility showed no significant association with epigenetic aging. However, chronic anxiety about declining physical health showed a strong, direct link to faster biological aging scores.

Subjective Fear Leaves an Objective Biomarker

Psychological health worries influence epigenetic modifications, the biological mechanisms that turn longevity genes on or off. Participants with higher health anxiety exhibited elevated DunedinPACE metrics, indicating that their bodies were aging faster than their chronological years predicted.

Epigenetic Clocks Are Dynamic and Modifiable

Because DunedinPACE measures biological speed rather than fixed damage, addressing the underlying stress signals and health behaviors associated with aging anxiety can help slow the clock back down.
