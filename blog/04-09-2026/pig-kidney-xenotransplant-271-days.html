Transplanted Pig Kidney Works in US Man's Body for Record 271 Days. Is This the Future?

A genetically modified pig kidney kept working inside a human patient for nine months — the longest a pig organ has ever survived in a person. Here's what happened, and what it means for the people still waiting.

A kidney transplanted from a genetically modified pig has functioned inside a human patient for 271 days — the longest a pig organ has ever worked in a person. The case, carried out by doctors at Mass General Brigham and just detailed in The Lancet, is being treated as a milestone for one of medicine's most closely watched experiments: using animal organs to solve a human organ shortage that kills people while they wait.

The Patient: Tim Andrews

Tim Andrews, 66, had end-stage kidney disease caused by type 2 diabetes. Before the transplant, his path forward looked bleak. In the US, roughly 100,000 people are waiting for a kidney, but only about 25,000 transplants happen each year. Andrews was told it could take seven years to reach the top of the list, and the average person survives only around five years on dialysis. In the UK, more than 7,000 people are currently on the kidney transplant waiting list.

Andrews described that math as its own kind of crisis, saying the wait "becomes very depressing." The offer of a pig kidney changed that. He called it his shot at freedom from the dialysis machine and a chance to actually live his life again.

Why Pigs, and Why It's Not as Simple as It Sounds

Pigs are considered the best candidate species for xenotransplantation — using organs from another species — because their organs are close to the right size for humans, and decades of pig farming already exist as infrastructure.

But a pig kidney can't just be transplanted as-is. The human immune system recognizes pig tissue as foreign almost instantly and launches a violent rejection response, the kind that can turn a transplanted organ black within minutes as its cells are destroyed and it clots from the inside.

To get around this, researchers engineered Yucatan miniature pigs with 69 separate genetic modifications. Broadly, these edits do three things: remove pig cell markers the human immune system is quick to attack, insert human DNA that helps the tissue blend in and avoid detection, and disable dormant viruses embedded in pig DNA to reduce infection risk.

How the Transplant Went

Andrews received his xenotransplant on January 25, 2025. According to the Lancet write-up, the kidney began functioning immediately. It kept working for 271 days before it had to be removed in October 2025.

271 days of function, no antibody activity found, and still no clear answer for why the kidney ultimately failed.

There were early warning signs of rejection, but doctors were able to manage that by adjusting his medication. Around the six-month mark, though, the blood vessels inside the kidney started to deteriorate and the organ became inflamed, eventually leading to failure. Notably, the medical team found no antibody activity attacking the kidney, meaning the exact cause of the decline still isn't fully understood.

After the pig kidney was removed, Andrews needed 82 days of dialysis before receiving a donor kidney in January of this year, which has been functioning well since.

What Doctors Are Taking Away From This

Dr. Leonardo Riella of Mass General Brigham's Center for Transplantation Sciences framed the broader organ shortage bluntly, describing it as a genuine crisis for patients without a living donor. His team's goal isn't to replace human donor transplants but to use pig organs as a bridge, buying patients time and potentially letting them skip dialysis altogether while they wait for a permanent match.

That's also the honest note the researchers ended on in their Lancet report: this case shows real promise for kidney xenotransplantation, but also makes clear the challenges haven't been solved yet.

The Bottom Line

271 days is a record, not a finish line. The pig kidney worked, then failed, and researchers still don't fully know why. But for a field where prior xenotransplant patients have had far shorter outcomes, this case is being read as proof that the approach can meaningfully extend a patient's time and quality of life while they wait for a human donor — which, given the scale of the kidney shortage, may be the difference between years of dialysis and years of an actual life.

Scientists proved how fear of health decline speeds up your aging with 700+ adults

A 2026 study led by researchers at the NYU School of Global Public Health evaluated data from 726 midlife adults to examine a counterintuitive question: Can worrying about getting older actually accelerate cellular aging?

By analyzing blood samples using advanced "epigenetic clocks," specifically DunedinPACE, which measures the real-time pace of biological aging, researchers discovered a direct, measurable link between psychological health anxiety and accelerated cellular decline.

Key Findings from the NYU Study

Not All Anxiety Ages You Equally

Researchers separated aging worries into three distinct categories: appearance, fertility, and health decline. Concerns regarding beauty and fertility showed no significant association with epigenetic aging. However, chronic anxiety about declining physical health showed a strong, direct link to faster biological aging scores.

Subjective Fear Leaves an Objective Biomarker

Psychological health worries influence epigenetic modifications, the biological mechanisms that turn longevity genes on or off. Participants with higher health anxiety exhibited elevated DunedinPACE metrics, indicating that their bodies were aging faster than their chronological years predicted.

Epigenetic Clocks Are Dynamic and Modifiable

Because DunedinPACE measures biological speed rather than fixed damage, addressing the underlying stress signals and health behaviors associated with aging anxiety can help slow the clock back down.
